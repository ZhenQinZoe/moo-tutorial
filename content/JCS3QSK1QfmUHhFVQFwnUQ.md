# Chapter 0: Overview (Kevin)

Modern machine learning (ML) systems are increasingly required to satisfy multiple, often competing, objectives at once, which can be examplified by the following examples: i) the post-training of large language models (LLMs) must balance helpfulness, harmlessness, and honesty while aligning with the diverse, and sometimes conflicting, preferences of different users (Ramé et al., 2023; Zhou et al., 2024b; Zhang et al., 2026b); ii) Machine unlearning must remove undesired knowledge without destroying a model's general utility (Li et al., 2024); iii) Federated learning must reconcile the quality of a global model with the needs of heterogeneous clients, each of which may care about a different set of tasks (Yang et al., 2023); and iv) Reinforcement learning (RL) agents in search and recommender systems must weigh clicks, watch time, conversions, and long-term user satisfaction rather than a single reward signal (Zhou et al., 2024a; Hairi et al., 2025). The conventional practice of collapsing all goals into one loss through a hand-tuned weighted sum is fragile. The weights are expensive to tune, they encode an implicit and rarely examined trade-off, and no choice of weights can reach Pareto-optimal solutions that lie on non-convex parts of the trade-off surface. To address these challenges, multi-objective optimization (MOO) provides a principled alternative. Instead of a single scalar loss, MOO considers the vector-valued problem 

$$\min_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^d}\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x}) := [f_1(\mathbf{x}),\ldots,f_S(\mathbf{x})]^\top,$$ 

and seeks *Pareto-optimal* solutions, i.e., points at which no objective can be improved without degrading another, together with the *Pareto front* that collects all such best-possible trade-offs. As trade-offs shift from afterthoughts to first-class design considerations, MOO is emerging as a foundation for next-generation ML and AI systems that must be capable, aligned, fair, and efficient at the same time.

The study of optimal trade-offs predates modern computing by more than a century. Its roots lie in economics, where Edgeworth (1881) and Pareto (1906) characterized allocations that cannot be improved for one party without hurting another, which is the origin of the term *Pareto optimality*. A rigorous mathematical theory followed in the mid-twentieth century: Kuhn and Tucker (1951) derived first-order optimality conditions for the vector maximum problem, Geoffrion (1968) introduced the notion of proper efficiency, and scalarization techniques, including the weighted-sum (Zadeh, 1963), $\epsilon$-constraint (Haimes et al., 1971), and weighted-Chebyshev (Steuer & Choo, 1983) methods, making MOO a mainstay and an important subfield of operations research and engineering design (Miettinen, 1999; Ehrgott, 2005). Beginning in the 1990s, population-based evolutionary algorithms such as NSGA-II (Deb et al., 2002) and MOEA/D (Zhang & Li, 2007) made it possible to approximate an entire Pareto front in a single run, but they scale poorly to the millions or billions of parameters in modern models. 

The bridge to deep learning was built by gradient-based MOO methods. Notably, the steepest-descent method of Fliege and Svaiter (2000) and the multiple-gradient descent algorithm (MGDA) of Désidéri (2012) compute, at each iteration, a common descent direction that decreases all objectives simultaneously, and they converge to *Pareto-stationary* points, the multi-objective counterpart of first-order stationarity. Since Sener and Koltun (2018) recast multi-task learning as MOO, the field has advanced rapidly on several fronts in recent years: gradient-manipulation methods that resolve conflicts among task gradients (Yu et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2021; Navon et al., 2022); preference-guided methods that target specific trade-offs or learn the entire Pareto set (Lin et al., 2019; Mahapatra & Rajan, 2020; Navon et al., 2021; Momma et al., 2022; Chen et al., 2024a); provably convergent stochastic algorithms that correct the bias of naïvely combining stochastic gradients (Fernando et al., 2023; Xiao et al., 2023); and theory that clarifies the three-way trade-off among optimization, generalization, and conflict avoidance (Chen et al., 2023). Today, MOO underpins multi-task vision and language models, multi-objective LLM alignment (Ramé et al., 2023; Zhou et al., 2024b), and industrial-scale learning-to-rank and recommendation (Zhou et al., 2023; Dong et al., 2026), and the area has matured enough to warrant dedicated surveys (Chen et al., 2025; Peitz & Hotegni, 2025).

Despite this progress, a significant gap remains between the classical MOO literature and the practice of modern ML. For example, classical MOO theory largely presumes deterministic, centralized, and moderately sized problems, whereas today's applications involve billions of parameters, noisy stochastic gradients, data dispersed across devices and organizations, nested decision hierarchies, and sequential interaction with an environment. These features raise key technical challenges that standard MOO methods do not address. (i) **Efficiency:** MGDA-type methods evaluate $S$ gradients and solve a quadratic subproblem at every iteration, so their per-iteration backpropagation cost grows linearly with the number of objectives, and their stochastic variants converge slowly and demand many samples. (ii) **Distributed data:** In federated and decentralized settings, data cannot be pooled, communication is the bottleneck, and clients may hold different objectives, so a common descent direction can no longer be computed centrally. (iii) **Hierarchical structure:** Hyperparameter optimization, meta-learning, data reweighting, and many LLM post-training pipelines are bilevel, so multiple upper-level objectives must be handled through hypergradients that depend on an inner problem that may lack strong convexity, and even the right notion of Pareto stationarity must be redefined. (iv) **Sequential multi-objective decision-making:** In multi-objective RL (MORL), gradients are estimated from correlated Markovian samples, which biases the weights of the common descent direction and raises open questions about sample complexity and the systematic exploration of Pareto-stationary policies. (v) **Preferences:** In many real-world applications, practitioners rarely want an arbitrary Pareto-optimal point. Rather, they want the one that matches a stated preference, or a faithful map of the whole Pareto front, both of which are costly to obtain in high dimensions. Existing tutorials and surveys (Chen et al., 2024b; Chen et al., 2025; Peitz & Hotegni, 2025) focus primarily on standard gradient-based MOO, and to our knowledge no resource yet offers a unified treatment of these emerging settings. This online tutorial blog is our attempt to fill that gap. Written as the companion website for our NeurIPS 2026 tutorial (<span style="color:red">link to be inserted</span>), this online tutorial blog aims to present a cohesive framework that connects core MOO principles (Pareto optimality and stationarity, scalarization, and multi-gradient methods) to the efficiency, distributed, hierarchical, and sequential challenges of contemporary AI, balancing intuition, algorithm design, and theoretical guarantees. Unlike a static survey, this online tutorial paper/blog is a *living document* that we will keep updating as this fast-moving field evolves.

The remainder of this online tutorial paper/blog is organized into six chapters:
* **Chapter 1 (Standard Multi-Objective Optimization)** establishes the foundations used throughout: Pareto optimality and Pareto stationarity, scalarization methods (linear and weighted-Chebyshev), MGDA and its stochastic variants, preference-guided exploration of the Pareto front, and the optimization–generalization–conflict-avoidance trade-off (Momma et al., 2022; Chen et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2024a). 
* **Chapter 2 (Efficiency)** tackles the computational and sample costs of gradient-based MOO. Section 2.1 covers acceleration through variance reduction and momentum, exemplified by the STIMULUS family of recursive multi-gradient estimators, which achieve $O(1/T)$ convergence for non-convex problems, exponential convergence for strongly convex ones, and state-of-the-art sample complexities (Liu et al., 2025). Section 2.2 covers "lazy" updates that recompute the multi-gradient weights only periodically, as in PSMGD, whose backpropagation complexity can be independent of the number of objectives (Xu et al., 2025). Section 2.3 reviews other low-complexity techniques. 
* **Chapter 3 (Distributed Multi-Objective Optimization)** takes MOO beyond a single machine. Section 3.1 presents federated multi-objective learning, in which clients with private data, and possibly different sets of objectives, collaboratively solve an MOO problem using algorithms such as FMGDA and FSMGDA (Yang et al., 2023). Section 3.2 turns to decentralized MOO over peer-to-peer networks without a central server. 
* **Chapter 4 (Multi-Objective Bilevel Optimization)** develops multi-objective bilevel learning (MOBL) for hierarchical decision-making with multiple upper-level objectives. It covers the weighted-Chebyshev multi-hyper-gradient-descent (WC-MHGD) method for finding preference-guided Pareto-stationary solutions and exploring the Pareto front (Zhang et al., 2026a), as well as a reformulation of multi-task bilevel learning as equality-constrained MOO that relaxes lower-level strong convexity to general convexity (Zhang et al., 2026b). 
* **Chapter 5 (Applications)** puts these tools to work. Section 5.1 treats MORL, from the MOAC actor-critic algorithm with the first finite-time Pareto-stationarity convergence and sample-complexity guarantees (Zhou et al., 2024a) to the weighted-Chebyshev actor-critic method MOCHA for systematically exploring Pareto-stationary policies, validated on a real-world short-video recommendation dataset (Hairi et al., 2025). Section 5.2 casts LLM unlearning as a trade-off between forgetting targeted knowledge and retaining general capabilities. Throughout, the chapter draws on industrial experience with multi-task search, ranking, and recommendation systems (Zhou et al., 2023; Dong et al., 2026). 
* Finally, **Chapter 6 (Future Directions and Open Problems)**, written jointly by the tutorial organizers and panelists, discusses open questions such as scaling to many objectives and foundation-model-sized problems, efficient and faithful Pareto-front exploration, and theory for distributed, hierarchical, and sequential MOO, and it invites the community to contribute to this evolving resource.
